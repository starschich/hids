\section{Solution of the load flow}
\label{sec:solution}

\subsection{Why the current is not explicit}
If both the power and the voltage were specified at the same end of the line,
the current would follow from a division. They are not: power is fixed at the
receiving end and voltage at the sending end, and the two are separated by a
loss that itself depends on the current. Substituting the target power into
Equation~\eqref{eq:flow} therefore gives a quadratic in $I_{d}$ rather than a
linear relation --- one that can be solved in closed form, so no iteration is
needed.

\subsection{The current}
Start from the DC power that must arrive at the inverter,
Equation~\eqref{eq:inv}:
\begin{equation}
  P_{dc,inv} = \frac{P_{ac,inv}}{1-p_{inv}} .
  \label{eq:step1}
\end{equation}
Insert it into Equation~\eqref{eq:flow} and collect terms:
\begin{equation}
  R_{loop}\cdot I_{d}^{\,2} - k\cdot U_{dpe,rec}\cdot I_{d} + P_{dc,inv} = 0 .
  \label{eq:quadratic}
\end{equation}
The operating point is the \textbf{lower} root:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  I_{d} = \frac{k\cdot U_{dpe,rec} - \sqrt{k^{2}\cdot U_{dpe,rec}^{2} - 4\cdot R_{loop}\cdot P_{dc,inv}}}{2\cdot R_{loop}}
  \;}
  \label{eq:current}
\end{equation}
with $U_{dpe,rec}$ in kV, $R_{loop}$ in $\Omega$, $P_{dc,inv}$ in MW and $I_{d}$ in
kA. Equation~\eqref{eq:current} covers both operating states through $k$. With
two poles energised ($k=2$) it simplifies to
\begin{equation}
  I_{d} = \frac{U_{dpe,rec} - \sqrt{U_{dpe,rec}^{2} - R_{loop}\cdot P_{dc,inv}}}{R_{loop}} .
  \label{eq:current2}
\end{equation}

Both roots of Equation~\eqref{eq:quadratic} deliver the same power to the
inverter, but they do it at different efficiencies. The upper root is the
high-current solution on the far side of the line's maximum power point, where
more current is pushed through the line only to burn the extra in $I^{2}\cdot R$. It
is not an operating point any converter control would settle at, and it lies
far outside the current rating of the conductors. The lower root is the
physical one.

\subsection{Maximum transferable power}
The square root in Equation~\eqref{eq:current} becomes imaginary when the
requested power exceeds what the line can deliver at that voltage. The limit
follows from $\mathrm{d}P_{dc,inv}/\mathrm{d}I_{d} = 0$ applied to
Equation~\eqref{eq:flow}:
\begin{equation}
  I_{d}\big|_{P_{max}} = \frac{k\cdot U_{dpe,rec}}{2\cdot R_{loop}},
  \qquad
  P_{max} = \frac{k^{2}\cdot U_{dpe,rec}^{2}}{4\cdot R_{loop}} .
  \label{eq:pmax}
\end{equation}
$P_{max}$ is a bound on solvability, not a capability: at that point half the
sending-end power is dissipated in the line. For any sensible design it sits
well above the rating, and a real link is limited by conductor thermal rating
and valve current long before it. Its use is as a sanity check --- if a
requested transfer has no solution, the combination of power, voltage and line
resistance is the reason. The outage state has one quarter the maximum of
balanced operation at the same voltage and, with the DMR in the loop, less than
that again.

\subsection{The rest of the flow}
With the current known, the remaining quantities follow directly:
\begin{align}
  P_{dc,rec}      &= k\cdot U_{dpe,rec}\cdot I_{d}                         &&\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:dcline}} \label{eq:c1}\\
  P_{loss,line}   &= R_{loop}\cdot I_{d}^{\,2}                   &&\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:dcline}, split by Eq.~\eqref{eq:losssplit}} \label{eq:c2}\\
  U_{dpe,inv}       &= U_{dpe,rec} - I_{d}\cdot R_{d,hv}                     &&\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:vinv}} \label{eq:c3}\\
  P_{ac,rec}      &= \frac{P_{dc,rec}}{1-p_{rec}}            &&\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:rect}} \label{eq:c4}\\
  P_{loss,total}  &= P_{ac,rec} - P_{ac,inv}                 &&\text{AC-to-AC loss} \label{eq:c5}\\
  \eta            &= \frac{P_{ac,inv}}{P_{ac,rec}}           &&\text{overall efficiency} \label{eq:c6}
\end{align}
The three loss contributions account for the whole difference between the two
ends:
\begin{equation}
  P_{ac,rec} - P_{ac,inv}
  = \underbrace{p_{rec}\cdot P_{ac,rec}}_{\text{rectifier}}
  + \underbrace{R_{loop}\cdot I_{d}^{\,2}}_{\text{line}}
  + \underbrace{p_{inv}\cdot P_{dc,inv}}_{\text{inverter}} .
  \label{eq:closure}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Current margin}
The margin does not enter the flow. It scales the operating current to a rating
figure for conductor selection, valve and bushing ratings, switchgear and
protection settings:
\begin{equation}
  I_{rated} = \bigl(1+m\bigr)\cdot I_{d}.
  \label{eq:margin}
\end{equation}
Losses, voltage drops and powers all belong to the operating current $I_{d}$,
not to $I_{rated}$; the margined value is a sizing quantity only. A worst-case
loss figure comes from re-evaluating the flow at a higher power, not from
substituting $I_{rated}$ into the loss relation, which would be inconsistent
with the stated transfer.

Because the outage state can carry the highest current of all the states
considered, the governing rated current for the conductor and the valves is the
largest $I_{rated}$ across them, not the one belonging to normal operation.
