\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

The link is represented by three elements in series: the rectifier station, the
DC line, and the inverter station. Each is a single algebraic relation.

\subsection{Conductor resistance and temperature}
Conductor tables quote DC resistance per unit length at a stated reference
temperature. The data used here is given at $T_{ref} = 20\,^{\circ}$C, while
the line in service runs hot, so the specific resistance is corrected to the
design conductor temperature $T$ before anything else is calculated.

The correction uses the inferred zero-resistance temperature $T_{0}$ --- the
temperature at which the linear resistance-temperature characteristic of the
metal extrapolates to zero:
\begin{equation}
  k_{T} = \frac{T_{0}+T}{T_{0}+T_{ref}},
  \qquad
  r'(T) = r'(T_{ref})\cdot k_{T} .
  \label{eq:ktemp}
\end{equation}
For hard-drawn 1350-H19 aluminium, which carries essentially all of the
current in an ACSR conductor, $T_{0} = 228\,^{\circ}$C. This is the same
statement as a temperature coefficient
$\alpha_{20} = 1/(228+20) = 0.00403\,\mathrm{K}^{-1}$ referred to
20\,$^{\circ}$C, written in the form that avoids having to restate $\alpha$
every time the reference temperature changes. The constant is a
property of the material, so a DMR of a different alloy is corrected with its
own value.

With the default design temperature of 75\,$^{\circ}$C --- the usual maximum
continuous operating temperature for an ACSR conductor --- the factor is
\begin{equation}
  k_{T} = \frac{228+75}{228+20} = \frac{303}{248} = 1.2218,
  \label{eq:ktvalue}
\end{equation}
that is, the line is some 22\,\% more resistive hot than the conductor table
suggests. Since line losses scale with resistance, ignoring the correction
would understate them by the same 22\,\%, which is why the temperature basis
belongs in the calculation rather than in a footnote.

The correction can be checked against the conductor table itself, which also
lists an AC resistance at 75\,$^{\circ}$C. For Bluebird,
$0.00801\cdot 1.2218 = 0.00979\,\Omega$/1000\,ft against a tabulated
$0.0105\,\Omega$/1000\,ft; the remaining 7\,\% is skin effect, which is why
the tabulated AC value is not the one to use on a DC line even though its
temperature is right.

\subsection{Line resistance}
The resistance of one pole conductor over the whole route is the corrected
specific resistance of a single subconductor, divided by the number of
subconductors in the bundle (they are electrically in parallel), converted to
$\Omega$/mi and multiplied by the length:
\begin{equation}
  R_{d\_hv} = \frac{r'_{d\_hv}\cdot 5.28}{n_{hv}}\cdot L\cdot k_{T},
  \qquad
  R_{d\_dmr} = \frac{r'_{d\_dmr}\cdot 5.28}{n_{dmr}}\cdot L\cdot k_{T}.
  \label{eq:resistance}
\end{equation}
The resistance that matters for the flow is that of the complete circuit ---
out and back --- which depends on the configuration:
\begin{equation}
  R_{loop} =
  \begin{cases}
    2\cdot R_{d\_hv}      & \text{symmetrical monopole, and balanced bipole,}\\[2pt]
    R_{d\_hv} + R_{d\_dmr}   & \text{bipole with one pole out (DMR return).}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:loop}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:resistance} is a DC resistance. No skin or proximity effect
is applied; on a DC line neither arises, and temperature is the one correction
that matters.

\subsection{Converter stations}
Station losses are taken as a fraction of the power passing through the
station. The two stations therefore differ only in which side of the
conversion is known:
\begin{align}
  \text{rectifier (AC}\rightarrow\text{DC):} \quad
    & P_{dc\_rec} = P_{ac\_rec}\cdot\bigl(1-p_{rec}\bigr)
      \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
      P_{ac\_rec} = \frac{P_{dc\_rec}}{1-p_{rec}},
      \label{eq:rect}\\[2pt]
  \text{inverter (DC}\rightarrow\text{AC):} \quad
    & P_{ac\_inv} = P_{dc\_inv}\cdot\bigl(1-p_{inv}\bigr)
      \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
      P_{dc\_inv} = \frac{P_{ac\_inv}}{1-p_{inv}}.
      \label{eq:inv}
\end{align}
A single fraction lumps everything inside the station fence: valve conduction
and switching losses, converter transformer and reactor losses, cooling and
auxiliary supply. The loss fraction is a straight percentage of throughput, so
it scales linearly with power and does not capture the no-load component of a
real loss curve; see Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}.

Because power is specified at the receiving AC terminals,
Equation~\eqref{eq:inv} is the \emph{entry point} of the whole calculation: it
converts the given $P_{ac\_inv}$ into the DC power that must arrive at the
inverter DC terminals.

\subsection{DC line}
With the DC voltage held at $U_{dpe\_rec}$ pole-to-ground at the rectifier, the power
leaving the rectifier and the resistive loss of the line are
\begin{equation}
  P_{dc\_rec} = k\cdot U_{dpe\_rec}\cdot I_{d},
  \qquad
  P_{loss\_line} = R_{loop}\cdot I_{d}^{\,2},
  \qquad
  k =
  \begin{cases}
    2 & \text{two poles energised,}\\
    1 & \text{one pole out,}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:dcline}
\end{equation}
and the power arriving at the inverter DC terminals is the difference:
\begin{equation}
  P_{dc\_inv} = k\cdot U_{dpe\_rec}\cdot I_{d} - R_{loop}\cdot I_{d}^{\,2}.
  \label{eq:flow}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:flow} is the load flow equation of the link. Everything in
Section~\ref{sec:solution} follows from solving it. The factor $k$ is the only
formal difference between a link running on two poles and one running on one:
with two poles energised the flow works against twice the pole-to-ground
voltage, with one pole out against $U_{dpe\_rec}$ alone, between the healthy
pole and the return.

The loss splits between the conductors in proportion to their resistances,
which is reported separately because the DMR contribution is what
distinguishes the outage case:
\begin{equation}
  P_{loss\_hv} =
  \begin{cases}
    2\cdot R_{d\_hv}\cdot I_{d}^{\,2} & \text{balanced,}\\
    R_{d\_hv}\cdot I_{d}^{\,2}  & \text{one pole out,}
  \end{cases}
  \qquad
  P_{loss\_dmr} =
  \begin{cases}
    0                    & \text{balanced,}\\
    R_{d\_dmr}\cdot I_{d}^{\,2}  & \text{one pole out.}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:losssplit}
\end{equation}
The two contributions must add to $R_{loop}\cdot I_{d}^{2}$ and no more: in the
one-pole-out case the HV term carries $R_{d\_hv}$ alone, not the loop resistance,
or the DMR loss is counted twice.

\subsection{Receiving-end voltage}
The voltage at the far end of the line is the sending-end voltage less the drop
along the conductors the current traverses. With two poles energised the
arrangement is symmetric --- each conductor drops $I_{d}\cdot R_{d\_hv}$ and the
midpoint stays at ground --- so the pole-to-ground voltage at the inverter is
\begin{equation}
  U_{dpe\_inv} = U_{dpe\_rec} - I_{d}\cdot R_{d\_hv},
  \label{eq:vinv}
\end{equation}
which is consistent with Equation~\eqref{eq:flow}, since
$2\cdot U_{dpe\_inv}\cdot I_{d} = 2\cdot U_{dpe\_rec}\cdot I_{d} - 2\cdot R_{d\_hv}\cdot I_{d}^{2}$.

With one pole out, Equation~\eqref{eq:vinv} still gives the pole-to-ground
voltage of the healthy pole at the inverter, but it is no longer the voltage
across the converter: the DMR carries the return current and rises by
$I_{d}\cdot R_{d\_dmr}$ above the rectifier neutral, so the converter sees
\begin{equation}
  U_{dpm\_inv} = U_{dpe\_rec} - I_{d}\cdot R_{loop}
  \qquad\text{(one pole out).}
  \label{eq:vinvmono}
\end{equation}
Both figures are useful --- the first for insulation coordination against
ground, the second for the converter operating point --- and the distinction
matters only in the outage case.
