\section{Scope and conventions}

\subsection{Purpose}
This document records the calculation process behind the \textbf{Load Flow} tab
of the HVDC calculation workbook. The tab answers one question for a
point-to-point HVDC link: \emph{given the AC power to be delivered at the
receiving end, what DC current flows, what voltage arrives at the receiving
converter, and what does the link cost in losses?} Two conductor arrangements
are covered --- the \textbf{symmetrical monopole} and the \textbf{bipole with a
dedicated metallic return (DMR)} --- together with the reduced-capacity
operating case of a bipole that has lost one pole.

The intent is a screening-level tool: closed-form, one operating point at a
time, transparent enough to check by hand, and consistent with the loss
calculation already carried in the \textbf{Losses} tab of the same workbook.
It is not a substitute for a network study; Section~\ref{sec:assumptions} lists
what is deliberately left out.

\subsection{Direction and reference conventions}
Power flows from the \textbf{rectifier} (sending station) to the
\textbf{inverter} (receiving station). Three conventions fix the problem and
are used throughout:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  \item \textbf{Power is defined at the receiving end}, at the AC terminals of
        the inverter station. This is the quantity a transmission service
        agreement or an interconnection request is written against, so it is
        the natural input; everything upstream --- current, line losses,
        sending-end power --- is then a result rather than an assumption.
  \item \textbf{DC voltage is regulated at the rectifier DC terminals.} The
        nominal pole-to-ground voltage $V_{d}$ holds at the sending station and
        the receiving-end voltage comes out lower by the resistive drop of the
        line.
  \item \textbf{Converter station losses are a fraction of station
        throughput}, applied per station, rather than a fixed number of
        megawatts.
\end{enumerate}

Conventions (1) and (2) sit at opposite ends of the line, which is what makes
the current an implicit rather than an explicit quantity: the sending-end power
depends on the line loss, the line loss depends on the current, and the current
depends on the sending-end power. Section~\ref{sec:solution} closes that loop
in one step instead of iterating.

\subsection{Units}
The calculation is carried in the engineering units the workbook uses, and the
unit set is self-consistent without conversion factors provided currents are
handled in kiloamperes:
\[
  [\mathrm{kV}]\times[\mathrm{kA}] = \mathrm{MW},
  \qquad
  [\Omega]\times[\mathrm{kA}]^{2} = \mathrm{MW}.
\]
Currents are \emph{reported} in amperes, which is why the spreadsheet formulas
carry factors of $1000$ and $1/1000$ around the current cell. Every equation in
this document uses $I_{d}$ in kiloamperes.

The one genuine conversion concerns the conductor data. Conductor tables give
DC resistance per 1000\,ft, and that is the form entered in the workbook, so the
factor
\[
  \frac{5280\ \mathrm{ft/mi}}{1000\ \mathrm{ft}} = 5.28
\]
converts specific resistance to $\Omega$/mi before multiplying by the line
length in miles.

{\color{atFgMuted}\footnotesize Note: the corresponding input row on the
\textbf{Losses} tab is labelled $\Omega$/mi, but the $5.28$ factor in its loss
formula shows the value entered there is in fact $\Omega$/1000\,ft (Bluebird
ACSR, $\approx 0.00801\,\Omega$/1000\,ft). The Load Flow tab labels the row
$\Omega$/1000\,ft; the label on the Losses tab should be corrected to match.}
